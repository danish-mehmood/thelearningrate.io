Training a model is mostly one idea repeated a few million times: look at how wrong you
are, figure out which way is <Annotate client:visible type="underline">downhill</Annotate>,
and take a small step that way. Do it enough and you slide into the valley.

<Figure caption="Each step points down the steepest slope — big steps up high, tiny steps near the bottom.">
  <LossCurve client:visible />
</Figure>

The curved line is the **loss** — a score for how wrong the model is. High up the walls
means very wrong; the <Annotate client:visible type="circle">bottom</Annotate> of the bowl
is as right as this model gets.

<MarginNote>
  <b>Why "small" steps?</b> Take too big a step and you overshoot the valley and bounce up
  the far wall. That step size has a name you'll meet everywhere: the <b>learning rate</b>.
</MarginNote>

## The loop, in four moves

<ol class="steps">
  <li><b>Measure.</b> Run the data through the model and score how wrong it is. That score is the <span class="ink-accent">loss</span>.</li>
  <li><b>Feel the slope.</b> The <span class="ink-blue">gradient</span> tells you which direction increases the loss fastest — so the opposite way is downhill.</li>
  <li><b>Step.</b> Nudge every parameter a little in the downhill direction. How far is the learning rate.</li>
  <li><b>Repeat</b> until the ground stops sloping — you've reached the bottom.</li>
</ol>

That's the whole engine. Everything fancier — momentum, Adam, schedulers — is just a
smarter way to choose *which way* and *how far* to step.
